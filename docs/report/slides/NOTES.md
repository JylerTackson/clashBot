## Script

### 1. Title (30 s)

Good afternoon, I am Tyler Jackson. Clashbot is an agent that plays Clash Royale in real time, from pixels. The talk is about how the course's knowledge-system ideas (retrieval, knowledge graphs, RAG and recommendation, agents) fit together in one working system. I will show the architecture, the numbers, including the ones where a simple baseline matches the LLM, and a short demo.

### 2. The problem (60 s)

The setting is deliberately hard. There is no game API: the iPhone is mirrored to a Mac, the bot sees only that window and acts only by clicking in it. A match is three minutes, elixir regenerates all the time, every play is irreversible, so decisions must come in well under a second; we are at 0.34 seconds median from frame to click. Perception is noisy: greyed cards read as unknown, and our live state agrees with the dataset builder at a feature cosine of only 0.67. The opponent's deck and hand are hidden, so we need prior knowledge, and that is where 197 pro games come in. A general LLM does not have that knowledge: the d1 model scores 0.22 on a four-way pick, below chance. Scope: Training Camp against the built-in trainer only, never ranked, in line with the game's terms of service; the executor needs two explicit switches and checks that the banner reads Trainer.

### 3. System overview (90 s)

Here is the whole system. Top row is the fast loop, running on every frame at about ten frames per second. Capture grabs the mirrored window; perception is two YOLOv8 detectors from KataCR plus OCR and a Kalman tracker; that becomes a live state, the same representation we built the dataset with. A small gate decides whether to act at all: reactive rules plus a LightGBM model, because pros wait about 80 percent of the seconds. If we act, a tactical engine, which runs a lane simulation to see how much tower damage each answer saves, and the knowledge graph propose and justify moves. The candidate generator merges those with retrieval from the pro database: kNN plus BM25 fused with reciprocal rank fusion, plus KG counters, to at most four options. The fine-tuned d1 language model picks one of the four, and the executor taps, behind a double guard. The tap changes the game and the loop closes. The dashed box is the slow loop, an optional Qwen coach that re-plans every twenty seconds; it is off in the showcase configuration, so everything you will see is the fast loop. Gold boxes are where the course material lives.

### 4. Data and knowledge (50 s)

The knowledge comes from 197 pro games in 33 videos by one creator, about twelve match-hours, 10,923 sampled states. We split by video, not by game, so no match leaks across train and test; that is the contamination lesson from the RAG lectures. The knowledge graph has two versions. Version 1 came from the wiki and knowledge base: 590 nodes. Version 2, which the live bot uses, has 840 nodes and 27,112 edges in 19 relations; the gold bars are what v2 added: lane-simulation edges such as STOPS and KILLS, deck co-occurrence, role transfer, and mined answer events, 3,620 cases where the opponent played X and a pro answered with Y.

### 5. Retrieval (L8-10) (65 s)

Retrieval, lectures 8 to 10. The live path is hybrid: exact kNN over a 644-dimensional structured state vector plus BM25 over the state text, fused by reciprocal rank fusion with k equals 60. On held-out pro states the channels are within noise of each other, hit at ten between 0.14 and 0.17, so a learned text embedding that costs twenty extra milliseconds is not worth it and is off. Left chart: HNSW at ef 40 reaches recall 0.978 in 0.31 ms against 1.5 ms exact, but at 10.9 thousand vectors exact search is already fast enough, so we do not use HNSW live. Right chart: filtering. A deck filter applied before the search leaves an empty pool for 89 percent of queries, and filtering after the ANN search at top-10 returns nothing for half of the deck queries and 87 percent of the phase queries; that is why the live path filters by legality, not by deck.

### 6. Knowledge graph and rules (L5-7) (65 s)

Knowledge graph, lectures 5 to 7. For link prediction I held out COUNTERS triples and compared four embedding models, equal tuning, three seeds. RotatE is the only one clearly above the popularity baseline: MRR 0.159 against 0.110; random is 0.022. Neuro-symbolic part: the lane simulation, top right, gives the engine a number to reason with: a lone level-15 balloon deals 3,162 tower hit points unanswered, 351 with a musketeer behind the tower, zero with a Tesla pull. Hard constraints then filter the options. Bottom left, the mined answer rules, held out by game: given an opponent play, rank the pro's deck for the answer. Pooled, 1,042 events, hit at one is 0.17 against 0.13 for random, significant (+4.4 pts, p < 0.001), but not better than plain train popularity (-0.6 pts, p = 0.65). A little real knowledge, but a popularity prior is as good. Perceived legality is noisy: the pros' own move passes our check only 54 to 60 percent of the time.

### 7. The ranker (L11-14) (100 s)

RAG plus recommendation, lectures 11 to 14, is the heart of the decision. It is a two-stage recommender: stage one retrieves at most four candidate plays, stage two is the language model picking one. To train and test it, each example is the pro's real play plus three hard negatives in a two-by-two grid: same card other placement, other card same placement, other card other placement. An earlier version of the negatives leaked the answer: a rule that looked only at the option keys scored 0.85. We found it, rebuilt the grid, and that rule is now 0.25 by construction. The chart is four-way accuracy on the test split, chance 0.25, with 95 percent confidence whiskers from a bootstrap over whole games. Zero-shot d1 is at chance, 0.22. Fine-tuned it reaches 0.48, with the tactics notes 0.51. A tuned LightGBM feature ranker reaches 0.48: the RecSys lesson that tuned simple baselines are competitive, measured on our data. The paired test says d1 with tactics against LightGBM is not significant (p = 0.26); the whiskers overlap. Against the placement prior, d1 is ahead by +12.3 pts, p < 0.001, which is real. The d1 variant with KG lines trained in was no better, it overfit: its KG lines on the train split came from the same pro games.

### 8. Agents (L15-18) (70 s)

Agents, lectures 15 to 18. The language model is the decision maker; the things around it act like tools already called: tactics lines, a few KG facts, notes from retrieved pro states, all rendered into d1's context. I also built typed read-only tools and a ReAct planner, but the Qwen planner made zero tool calls, so the results are precomputed. Execution is separate and guarded: two switches, the Trainer banner, a read hand, and a cooldown after failed taps. Taps landed 75/84 = 89% and 78/95 = 82% in the two newest live runs. Top right, knowledge editing: a synthetic plus-one-elixir change on five cards; the fact store updates answers and leaves unrelated ones alone. That shows the mechanism, not a real patch. Bottom right, the context-conflict test: I corrupt the notes in d1's context, test split. Accuracy stays between 0.49 and 0.52 in every condition, and d1 follows a poisoned note only 4% of the time. The fine-tuned d1 barely reads its context: wrong notes do not hurt, and right ones add little.

### 9. Bot vs pros on videos (60 s)

To measure decisions without playing, I run the full live stack on test-split pro videos, open loop: 8 of 24 games so far, so these numbers are preliminary. Bot taps are matched to pro plays within two seconds. Timing: 78% (73-85%) of pro plays have a bot tap nearby, and 74% (70-79%) of bot taps have a pro play nearby. Read that against the null, the same taps shifted twenty to sixty seconds: 70% (66-76%) and 67% (63-71%). The bot simply taps more, 23.5 against 16.9 a minute. Choice: same card as the pro 17% (12-21%), against 13% (10-16%) for a random card from its perceived hand. The ceiling explains why: the pro's card is in the perceived hand only 40% of the time. Left, an agreement; right, the bot defends with another card. Open loop and hidden information: this is agreement, not quality.

### 10. Live results (60 s)

Live results, Training Camp. The latency chart is three repeated dry runs on one pro video, mean and standard deviation: perception is the biggest stage, then the d1 ranker. The decision totals 272 plus or minus 20 milliseconds median and 455 plus or minus 41 at the 95th percentile, so the 300 millisecond p95 target is missed in all three runs. Dry runs do not click; live, the two clicks add 183 milliseconds, and capture to click was 339 median, 572 at the 95th. Taps land mostly, not perfectly: 75/84 = 89% and 78/95 = 82% in the two newest live runs, failures concentrate on Tesla and fireball; an older run landed 95 of 100. Trial results: [Training Camp record from the red tile]. The first win came in overtime in game three of one run; with ten games the 95 percent interval is very wide, so I will not call it a win rate.

### 11. Comparison with prior bots (50 s)

Two prior systems. KataCR, Wu et al., is an offline-RL transformer imitating one human, reporting five percent, 1 of 20, against the built-in AI for its headline model and zero to ten percent across nine models; we reuse its detector weights. Claude Royale is a Claude agent that plays screenshots through a vision model on the ladder, about seven seconds per decision and no built-in-AI win rate. Ours differs by design: it carries a pro-play database, a knowledge graph and a simulation engine, and takes 0.34 seconds. The protocols differ in deck, opponent version and sample size, so read the win-rate row as context, not as a benchmark.

### 12. Demo (clip or screen share) (100 s)

[Play the 60-second demo clip or switch to the live screen share.] While it plays, point at the dashboard numbers. One: the ten pro states most like this situation. Two: up to four candidate plays with d1's probability as the bar, here 88 against 12 percent. Three: the reason, from the tactics engine, here that a Tesla pull to the centre saves about 500 tower hit points. Four: the knowledge graph, the counters the candidate generator used and the facts given to d1. If anything breaks live, the recorded clip is the fallback.

### 13. Lessons and future work (40 s)

What worked: knowledge plus fine-tuning moved a chance-level LLM to about 0.51, and the guarded executor lands most of its taps. What I would be honest about: simple baselines match the LLM; perception noise caps everything, the pro's own move passes our legality check only 54 to 60 percent of the time and the hand is fully read in about a third of moments; and n is small, wins are counted in tens. Next: elixir management, since in the last run about a third of match frames had no affordable card; retrain with the KG-leak fixed; more Training Camp games with intervals. Thank you; questions.

## File format notes

- The deck is plain 13.333 x 7.5 in (no `type=` on `p:sldSz`), text boxes, rectangles, connectors, tables and PNG pictures only (Keynote, Google Slides and PowerPoint friendly). No zero-extent shapes, no palette PNGs (logos are converted to RGBA copies `assets/logos/*_rgba.png`; the downloaded originals are kept).
- Speaker notes are NOT embedded by default: python-pptx's notes master makes macOS Quick Look hang and Keynote refuse the file. This file is the script. `python build_slides.py --with-notes` embeds it anyway (for PowerPoint users).
- The footer (gold bar, UCF logo left, CECS logo right, slide-number field) is drawn on every slide, not in the master, so Google Slides import keeps it; the slide number is a real slide-number field.
- The script writes to `clashbot_showcase.pptx.tmp` and renames it into place after the checks.
